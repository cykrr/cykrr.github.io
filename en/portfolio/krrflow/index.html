krrflow: a 3D music shelf for the couch.

Case study · Rust · Windows · Private code for now. Under active development since late October 2026.

A local music player that swaps the file list for a Cover Flow-style carousel on a reflective floor. Albums are arranged like records in a rack, by artist or by decade, with a HUD built to be read from ten feet away.

the idea

Playing music on a PC wired to the TV usually means a desktop player with tiny buttons. krrflow starts from the other end: the centred album is the disc. Press play and it ejects onto the turntable and spins, while progress, the track list and transport work from the keyboard, the mouse wheel or a gamepad.

Importing needs no file manager: pick a folder from the HUD or drag one onto the window. Each folder containing audio becomes an album, with folder names as the fallback when tags are missing.

what is in it

  • GPU-drawn: A 3D scene on wgpu (DirectX 12 on Windows, with fallback to other backends). Covers, floor reflection, the procedural CD and the backdrop are drawn with WGSL shaders; optional 4× MSAA.
  • Gapless playback: The album's next track is opened ahead of time, so track changes leave no silence in between.
  • Adaptive frame pacing: With nothing moving it draws no frames at all; while the disc spins it drops to 60 Hz; it runs at the full monitor rate only while the carousel is moving.
  • Track list: A side panel with the current track highlighted, click to play, and missing files marked in a different colour.
  • Gamepad: D-pad or stick to change album, A to play or pause, B to stop, and the bumpers to skip tracks.
  • Seek and volume: Click the progress bar to jump anywhere. Sound effects are subtle, and auto-advance is silent.

engineering decisions

A ring of textures, not a texture per album.

The carousel draws about thirteen covers at once. Instead of uploading every cover to the GPU, I keep a ring of 32 layers at 1024×1024 that get recycled as you scroll. A library of any size fits in roughly 128 MiB, and startup only decodes what the first frame shows.

Nothing heavy on the frame thread.

Reading, decoding and resizing cover art happens on a worker thread, and GPU uploads have a per-frame budget, so a long jump spreads over a few frames instead of causing a hitch. SQLite was also moved off the render loop.

A content-addressed pool.

Cover art is copied into a pool indexed by BLAKE3 hash, so the shelf keeps its artwork even if the source folder moves. Audio is referenced where it lives by default; --copy stores it in the pool too.

Gapless playback.

When a track starts, the engine already opens the next track's decoder and, when the current one ends, chains its audio right behind the pending buffer without resetting the queue. The progress bar counts frames the sound card actually consumed, not a timer, so it flips exactly when the new track is heard. The engine is its own crate, tested against synthetic WAVs.

Draw only when needed.

On every pass the event loop decides whether to draw now, at 60 Hz, or not at all, depending on what is animating: a moving carousel, the spinning disc, cover loads or a scan. At rest it waits for input and leaves the CPU and GPU free, which matters on a living-room PC that plays for hours.

Caring about the look.

Custom shaders for the CD (metallic zones and radial diffraction), cover rims with analytic anti-aliasing, a floor reflection that rotates with the cover, and a 4× MSAA toggle. Each group's divider card is painted with a built-in bitmap font.

Measure before guessing.

A developer build ships per-frame CPU and GPU profiling, attributes slow frames to the gap between frames rather than only render(), and shows why the event loop is awake.

how it is built

A Rust workspace of five crates (protocol, core, audio, UI and CLI), around 14,000 lines and over 150 tests. CI on Ubuntu runs fmt, clippy -D warnings and the full suite. Technical debt lives in a register with priority and effort per item, under the rule of paying it down when it blocks a feature, not in a big-bang rewrite. Product target: Windows 10/11 on DirectX 12, with Vulkan as a fallback.
